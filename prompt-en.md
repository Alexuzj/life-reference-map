# Life Reference Map — English Prompt

Copy everything below and paste it into the AI you normally use.

---

```text

I feel uncertain about my current situation or future direction. I do not need you to tell me what to do. I want to find real people worth studying so that their paths can expand my sense of what may be possible for me.

Guide me through a “Life Reference Map” exercise.

## Your task

Use the least amount of real evidence needed to understand how I may want to work and live, what “a path that works” means to me, and which trade-offs I am willing to make. Then suggest three to five people worth exploring.

They are not idols or definitive answers. They are points of reference that can help me think.

The final output should be a candidate list, brief reasons for each match, and starting points for further research—not a complete report on each person's life. Your job is to help me find people worth exploring, not to replace the research I should do afterward.

## Stage 1: Understand me

Ask one question at a time and choose the next question based on my answer. Do not show all questions at once, and do not recommend anyone too early.

Ask for specific experiences rather than abstract self-descriptions. Do not focus only on my résumé, job title, or general interests.

Begin with this question:

“What recent, specific experience made you question your current direction or feel drawn toward a different way of working and living? What happened?”

If I say “I don't know,” reframe the question or offer one or two angles that may help me remember. Do not answer for me.

Gradually uncover:

1. What I genuinely want to change about my current situation.
2. Which decision I most want to clarify in the next 6–12 months—or, if there is no specific decision yet, which direction I most want to test.
3. What activities made me engaged, energized, or willing to invest time during the past year.
4. What people regularly ask me for help with, and where I have created real value.
5. What kinds of work or lives I envy, and what exactly attracts me about them.
6. If I believed I was on the right path three years from now, what two pieces of evidence would matter most: income, stability, autonomy, mastery, impact, lifestyle, or something else? What minimum practical conditions must be met?
7. Which trade-offs I am willing to make, and which constraints or boundaries I will not sacrifice—including time, financial runway, location, care responsibilities, and barriers to entry.
8. Which languages I can use for reading or listening.

If an answer is abstract, ask for a concrete example. If you already have enough evidence, do not repeat a question just to complete a script.

After an important answer, you may reflect the main point in one or two sentences. Do not turn every exchange into a long analysis.

Usually complete the interview in five to eight questions. Move on only when you can explain my motivations, demonstrated strengths, definition of success, aspirations, and constraints with concrete evidence. If an important gap remains after eight questions, explain what is missing and ask whether I want to continue. Do not exceed ten questions without my agreement.

I may say “give me a first version” at any time. Continue with the evidence available and state the uncertainty clearly. I may also say “keep exploring.”

Do not assume that I understand myself perfectly. Watch for contradictions such as:

- I claim to love something but have not invested time or effort in it. First distinguish imagined interest from lack of opportunity or a real constraint. Missing behavioral evidence does not make the interest false, but it should lower your confidence.
- I envy someone's fame, wealth, or lifestyle rather than their actual work.
- I want conditions that may conflict with each other.
- I describe who I think I should become rather than who I am willing to become.

When you notice a contradiction, point it out kindly but directly. Do not flatter me.

## Stage 2: Build my reference profile

After the questions, do not recommend anyone yet.

Briefly separate what you have heard into four categories:

- **Supported by evidence**: things I have actually done, sustained, or created value through
- **Still an aspiration**: things I want but have not yet supported with behavior
- **Still unknown**: missing information that could change the candidates
- **Another plausible explanation**: whether a different interpretation also fits the evidence

Then propose three to five concrete selection criteria covering how I want to work and live, income model, transition path, realistic starting point, and trade-offs. Avoid vague qualities such as “successful,” “creative,” or “influential.”

Ask one reality-check question: “If you actually tried this direction, what outcome would make you reconsider it?”

Ask me to confirm or correct your understanding. Continue only after I confirm it.

## Stage 3: Suggest three to five people

Search globally across countries, industries, genders, and historical periods. Do not default to famous English-speaking figures or people from my own language and culture.

Choose people for the relevance of the underlying pattern of how they built their work and life, not their fame.

First compare a broader candidate pool against the selection criteria. Do not reveal lengthy internal reasoning; show only the final shortlist. Compare their starting conditions, transition, income model, daily work, and trade-offs—not only what they eventually achieved.

Aim for a varied shortlist:

- Two people whose paths are highly relevant
- One person whose original starting point or practical conditions were closer to mine
- One person from an adjacent path who offers a useful new angle
- Optionally, one person who reveals the costs, a counterexample, or a more realistic alternative

Do not sacrifice relevance to reach five names. Recommend only people with enough reliable public material to trace their important choices. If you cannot find enough, say so.

Do not use a wide table. Give each person a short profile card:

- **Why this person**: which criteria and evidence make the match relevant
- **Turning point to study**: a choice, transition, or income model worth tracing
- **How the work and income fit together**: their main work, income sources, and how multiple interests connect; what is verified and what remains unclear
- **Where the comparison breaks down**: era, family background, capital, identity, luck, access, or barriers to entry
- **Starting resource**: the best place to begin
- **Verification status**: which statements are verified facts and which are your inferences

Prioritize first-person interviews, talks, books, podcasts, reliable biographies, and authoritative sources.

Call a source “verified” only if you actually opened it during this conversation and confirmed that it supports the relevant claim. Search snippets do not count.

Before recommending people, state whether you can actually search for and open webpages in this conversation. If you cannot open pages, follow the “provisional candidates” rules and do not pretend that verification occurred.

If browsing is available, provide at least one verified starting source for each first-round candidate. For each final core reference, provide at least one first-person source and one reliable independent source.

If browsing is unavailable, label the entire list “provisional candidates.” Do not generate specific links, book titles, podcast episodes, or interviews from memory. Give search keywords instead and state that the candidates require verification once search is available.

## Stage 4: Filter quickly

After presenting the candidates, ask me to react to everyone in one reply and briefly explain why:

- Interested: I want to study this person further
- Not interested: The path may be relevant, but the person or direction does not attract me
- Too familiar: This person offers little new perspective
- I disagree: I think your reasoning is wrong
- Unsure: I need more information

Help me distinguish between a genuinely irrelevant path, simple unfamiliarity with the person, disagreement with your factual judgment, and lack of interest in their real daily work. Do not immediately replace a highly relevant but unfamiliar person; first give me a short starting resource.

Use my feedback to adjust or replace candidates, keep two or three core reference figures, and ask me to choose one person to study first. Do not keep weak candidates just to reach a quota.

For each final person, give me:

- One starting resource
- One low-cost research action I can complete in the next week
- One hypothesis to test
- One question I should answer after studying them

Research actions must remain focused on learning about the person and testing assumptions. Do not directly recommend quitting a job, starting a company, investing money, relocating, or making another major life decision.

## Rules you must follow

- Treat every candidate as a hypothesis to test, not the correct answer to my life.
- Do not assume a path was inevitable simply because it eventually succeeded.
- Do not confuse fame with relevance.
- Look beyond people with the same job title; search for people with a similar life structure.
- Test my self-description against behavioral evidence, real constraints, and contradictions.
- Never invent biographical details or sources.
- Keep the questions clear and the output concise, but do not skip information that would materially affect the result just to finish quickly.
- Do not require my real name, employer name, exact income, or other identifying details. Let me use general descriptions and ranges when discussing practical constraints.
- Do not present this exercise as a psychological diagnosis, career assessment, or certain prediction.
- Your goal is not to find the “correct role model.” Based on the evidence I have provided so far, generate a shortlist that is verifiable, falsifiable, and worth further study.

Begin now. Ask only one question at a time.
```
